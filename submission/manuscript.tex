\hypertarget{introduction}{%
\subsection{Introduction}\label{introduction}}

A persistent challenge in amplicon and shotgun metagenomic sequencing of
microbial communities is the variation in the number of reads obtained
across samples. This challenge is not unique to microbial communities
and has long plagued ecologists, in general. The classical approach to
control for uneven sampling is rarefaction. In rarefaction, a consistent
number of individuals are randomly sampled from each sample. An alpha or
beta diversity metric is then calculated from the sampling. The random
subsampling and diversity measurement steps are repeated a large number
of times (e.g., 100 or 1,000) and the measurements are averaged over the
subsamples. Unfortunately, the term ``rarefying'' and occasionally
rarefaction have been misdefined as a single subsampling step.
Regardless, over the past 10 years, the process of rarefaction has
become controversial because some view the choice of the sampling depth
as arbitrary, the metrics can become more similar than they truly are,
and because the practice of downsampling to a common number of sequences
leaves the impression that good data are being unnecessarily discarded.
Numerous alternatives have been proposed including calcuting the
relative abundance of each taxon in in a community so that they sum to 1
within a sample, normalization of counts so that the total number of
individuals sums to a user defined value, use of estimators of diversity
that can extrapolate beyond the number of observed individuals, and the
use of compositional statistics, which are based on a log ratio to a
reference value.

Researchers have adopted compositional methods because the number of
sequences obtained per sample is typically independent of the true
number of individuals in a community and so the number of times each
taxon is observed is not independent of the other taxa. The general
approach is to calculate a centralized log ratio (i.e., CLR) where the
abundance of each taxon within a sample is divided by the geometric mean
abundance within the sample. This ratio is then used as the argument for
the natural logrithm. These values are then used to calculate pairwise
euclidean distances between samples resulting in so-called Aitchison
distances. Aside from controlling for the compositional nature of
sequence data, proponents of the Aitchison distance claim that the
distances are insensitive to differences in sampling effort. A challenge
of Aitchison distances is that because microbial communities are patchy
a large number of features will have a frquency of zero in each sample.
This will cause the geometric mean to be undefined. To overcome this
challenge, factors are added to the data. Three approaches have been
proposed: adding a pseudocount of 1 all frequency data (i.e., Nudge
CLR), adding a pseudocount of 1 divided by the total number of
individuals in the sample (i.e., Nudge CLR), and imputing zero values to
be a random small value (i.e.~Zero CLR). As an alternative to these
transformations, Martino et al.~{[}\textbf{XXXXX}{]} proposed
calculating CLR values for each sample after ignoring zero values
followed by matrix completion or robust Principal Components Analysis
(i.e., RPCA). The authors claim that the advantages of RPCA over the
other CLR-based approaches is that it does not require imputing zero
values and that the method is less sensitive to uneven sampling effort.

In contrast to these claims, my previous work has shown that the output
of RPCA and other alternatives to rarefaction are more sensitive to
uneven sampling than rarefaction. Futhermore, use of these methods can
inflate the false detection rate and decrease the statistical power to
detect differences in alpha and beta diversity metrics. Given the near
unanimous agreement over the past decade that rarefaction is
``inadmissible'' and the development of alternatives to rarefaction, one
might question why my results disagree so markedly with those of others.
There are several possible reasons. First, researchers often propose new
methods by demonstrating their method reveals new insights over old
methods without actually benchmarking the method. Because one cannot
know the true difference between samples, a positive or negative result
with experimental data cannot be interpreted. Second, when bencharking
tests are developed, researchers may inadvertantly select tests that
favor the method they are developing. This was the case in my recent
re-analysis of the original paper that called rarefaction into question.
Therefore, it is necessary to use a comprehensive benchmarking test
suite that allows one to compare diverse methods using inference methods
that are commonly used by researchers. Third, the choice of how to
simulate community data can indadvertantly favor the method being
developed. A popular cliche states that ``all models are wrong, some are
useful''. Similar to one's choice of benchmarking tests, the modelling
approach may favor some methods over others. For instance, I have
critiqued how others have accounted for variation in sampling depth as
being unlike what we see in experimental data.

Previously, I generated null community models and treatment groups with
known effect sizes by creating taxon abundance distributions and
sampling depths based on 12 published datasets. Alternatively, Martino
et al.~modelled communites with known effect sizes using a statistical
model. This difference in modelling strategy has created an interesting
opportunity to reframe the question of whether to use rarefaction and to
reassess the performance of RPCA and other CLR-based approaches.
Although Martino et al., used an interesing modelling approach, they did
not compare RPCA to other CLR-based approaches or to rarefaction.
Furthermore, they used benchmarking tests that did not reflect how
researchers typically analyze microbial community data. Therefore, the
goal of the current study was to reassess RPCA and other CLR-based
methods to rarefaction using the modelling and benchmarking framework
used by Martino et al.

\hypertarget{simulated-communities---overview}{%
\subsubsection{Simulated communities -
Overview}\label{simulated-communities---overview}}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Used code for simulations found in the deicode benchmarking github
  repository
\item
  Describe simulations as indicated in paper

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Relative abundance of features described by following a normal
    distribution with mean and standard deviation determined by fitting
    data from a hand microbiome study
  \item
    Add random amount of noise to each relative abundance value
  \item
    Add random amount of noise to subset of relative abundance values
  \item
    Used Poisson-lognormal distribution to sample frequency of each
    feature in each sample to obtain overall desired level of sampling
    effort for each sample
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Found numerous problems with simulations as coded

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Only one replicate of each sequencing depth rather than 10 or 100
  \item
    the axis labels in original heatmap (Fig 3DE) were switched
  \item
    Overlapped samples rather than features
  \item
    Variation in feature abundance was across samples rather than across
    features
  \item
    Homoscedastic and heteroscedastic variation and creating sparsity
    was screwed up - didn't actually subsample, signs of negative values
    were removed
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Rewrote simulation code and analyzed original simulation data in
  parallel to revised data (except for PERMANOVA power analysis because
  it was not possible to get treatment groups to truly overlap with each
  other)

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Simulated communitites had uneven sampling effort, increased
    sampling effort and then subsampled to correct depth
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Qualitatively was able to reproduce the original results
\end{itemize}

\hypertarget{simulated-communities---permanova-analysis}{%
\subsubsection{Simulated communities - PERMANOVA
analysis}\label{simulated-communities---permanova-analysis}}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Used PERMANOVA F-statistic rather than p-values (why?!), likely
  because even MatrixCompletionon raw counts was was significant
\item
  Using rclr transformed data outperformed raw data when followed by
  matrix completion
\item
  F statistic values were inflated because they only used 2 dimensions,
  which artificailly cut out a lot of noise that would bring down the F
  statistic values
\item
  Investigators compared results using overlapped community and found
  RPCA outperformed MatrixCompletion; didn't compare to Bray-Curtis,
  rarefaction, other compositional methods
\item
  Noted that they did not include other relevant comparisons such as
  using relative abundance, other compositional transformations,
  rarefaction, subsampling, etc. Expande workflow pipeline to include
  these other methods
\item
  Found numerous problems with simulations

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Desired to increase number of replicate datasets from 10 to 100 to
    test statistical power
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Rewrote simulation code and selected a level of overlap and a total
  number of features so that approximately 50\% of tests using 4000
  sequences would have been significant when using rarefaction
\item
  Bray-Curtis and OCLR did as well as each other; much better than RPCA
  and other methods
\item
  Researchers used balanced designs with varying number of samples per
  treatment group. Unnecessary other than to show the change in power
  with sampling effort. Likely done because knn analysis is sensitive to
  uneven smapling effort. Regardless, the results did not change
\end{itemize}

\hypertarget{simulated-communities---knn-analysis}{%
\subsubsection{Simulated communities - KNN
analysis}\label{simulated-communities---knn-analysis}}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Unaware of researchers who have used KNN to differentiate communities.
  Far more likely that they would use PERMANOVA or another machine
  learning technique like logistic regresssion or random forest.
\item
  Problems with analysis

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Only generated one simulation for each sequencing depth - would have
    done 10 to test sensitivity on RNG seed
  \item
    Did not run knn on negative and positive control samples - included
    all three simulation datasets
  \item
    Said they did 40:60 training-testing splits, but actually did 60:40
    training-testing splits - would have prefered 80:20 training-testing
    splits
  \item
    Used a single seed per simulation replicate - modified to use 100
    seeds to generate 100 80:20 splits (did they say they used 1 or 10;
    pretty sure code only was for 1 iteration)
  \item
    Hard coded k value - would have preferred to use a variety of values
    in the training with the optimal value selected for the testing
    phase
  \item
    Only used raw and rclr transformed data as input to matrix
    completion followed by PCA and used that as input to kNN. Later,
    they used weighted UniFrac and Bray-Curtis ordinations to generate
    distances - would have preferred to use the distances themselves as
    input
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Regardless of how the training-testing splits were generated or
  whether their simulated data or mine were used rarefaction performed
  as well or better than all other methods, including RPCA.
\item
  Across the board, as shown in original paper, the classification
  accuracy increased with sampling effort
\item
