Dear Susannah,

I have serious concerns about the reviews that were obtained for my manuscript (mSystems01826-25). The reviews to be superficial without commenting on the actual results I presented. I apologize in advance for the length of this note!

I know that authors often complain that reviewers ask for things that were included in a manuscript or that they are asking the author to write a different paper. But in this case, I am convinced that the reviewers did not read the paper to understand that it was an effort reproduce the original work of Martino and reanalyze Martino's RPCA method for handling uneven sampling effort. They go on to ask about feature selection, metagenomics, batch correction, and variation in rrn copy number. These are not germane to this paper, much less the question of whether rarefaction versus compositional methods should be used.

The reviewers also criticized my use of methods for conducting the benchmarking. For example, they criticized me for using accuracy when there was uneven sampling effort with the KNN method. This is similar to a criticism that I levied against Martino et al. Martino used accuracy as their metric for assessing KNN. I was reproducing what they had done. If the reviewers had read the paper, they would have seen that balanced subsets were used to remove the balancing (except that what Martino did originally had an error in it). Similarly, I used PERMANOVA because it was used by Martino. I did not pursue feature selection because Martino's RPCA approach is not compatible with feature selection methods.

There were also comments that did not align with what I wrote or what was in the literature. For example, Reviewer 1 stated that "statement in line 155 appears overly critical". The statement at line 155 was actually positive - It stated that I was able to reproduce what Martino had done. Elsewhere, they state, "Some researchers view compositional transformations as an alternative to rarefaction rather than a preprocessing step that must be applied after rarefaction". But nowhere in the manuscript do I suggest using compositional and rarefaction methods together. Later they also state, "Reduced performance in low–effect size settings may reflect the well-known trade-off associated with log-ratio transformations, where variance stabilization and compositional correction can come at the cost of statistical power, particularly in sparse microbiome data". There are no citations for the "well-known trade-off". If anything, McMurdie & Holmes argued that rarefaction compromises statistical power relative to other the methods mentioned by the reviewer.

Finally, I was struck that both reviewers missed the context of the manuscript and did not seem to have any concern that a paper originally published in mSystems had significant problems. Frankly, given the problems in their code, I wonder whether Martino should be retracted. Yet, neither reviewer thought my manuscript was in scope at mSystems. The second reviewer suggested that I "change the article type from "Research Article" to "Methods and Protocols" or "Opinions/Hypotheses". Again, this makes no sense given the context of what I did in my manuscript.

I did my best to respond to the reviewers' comments in my rebuttal and in making revisions to the manuscripts. There were several comments that I was grateful to receive. One comment allowed me to uncover another serious problem in Martino's benchmarking analysis using UniFrac. I typically bend over backwards to satisfy reviewers. In this case, I felt the majority of the comments were off base and out of scope for my study.

Given the superficial nature of the reviews and the comments that did not align with the text or the literature, I question whether they were produced using generative AI tools. I know that AI detection software is limited and often gives false positives and false negatives. It is also easy for authors to blame an unfavorable result on AI. Regardless of whether AI was used, I hope you'll agree that these reviews were strikingly bad. I have already raised these reviews to the ASM Journal Ethics team who commented that AI use requires reviewers to self-disclose their use of the tools. I really think the reviewers should be asked directly about whether they used generative AI tools in their reviews. I would love to be wrong about the use of AI tools in these reviews.

I know reviewers can have bad days. Again, regardless of whether AI tools were used, these reviews were not substantive and fall short of what I am used to receiving from ASM's reviewers.

Sincerely,
Pat Schloss
